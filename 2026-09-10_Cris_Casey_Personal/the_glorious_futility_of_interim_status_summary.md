## Summary (10% Word Count)

The essay argues that project status tracking, despite three decades of investment in tools, certifications, and methodologies, has failed to improve delivery outcomes.

**The Problem with Plans:** Project plans rest on two falsehoods: duration estimates are backward-engineered from arbitrary deadlines rather than discovered through honest assessment, and dependency relationships capture only formal sequences while ignoring the soft constraints (office politics, vendor availability, undocumented sign-offs) that actually govern execution. When reality diverges from the plan -- which happens immediately -- the organization updates the plan and regenerates the chart, calling this "management." It resembles revising yesterday's weather forecast after checking the window.

**The Apparatus:** Enterprise PM tools charge $9-$54 per user monthly. A 50-person team spends $54,000-$130,000 annually maintaining records of how badly they're keeping to a wrong plan. Project managers dedicate 150+ hours yearly to status reports describing plans that stopped being true weeks ago. Meanwhile, the actual project state lives in Slack threads, standups, and hallway conversations -- places the plan never sees. The tool promises real-time visibility; it delivers a real-time view of last week's outdated input.

**PMI's Own Verdict:** Only 18% of PMs demonstrate high business acumen; that 18% achieves 27% lower failure rates and better schedule adherence -- distinguished by organizational instinct, not plan maintenance. The other 82% produce artifacts. A 2024 survey found PMOs fail to demonstrate business value 93% of the time.

**What Research Shows:** The Standish Group found 16.2% on-time, on-budget delivery in 1994; by 2020 (after three decades of methodology, tools, certifications), it was 31% -- half a percentage point per year. McKinsey: large IT projects run 45% over budget and deliver 56% less value than promised. Small projects succeed ~90% of the time; large ones <10%. Projects over $10 million are 10× more likely to be canceled than those under $1 million. Bigger plan, bigger apparatus, worse outcomes.

**The Only Date That Matters:** In private enterprise, the only commitment that counts is the delivery date you keep. Every other date -- baseline, revised baseline, re-baselined after scope changes -- is either history or fiction. The machinery tracking their evolution (baseline revisions, variance reports) exists to explain why you missed the promised date while sounding like process rather than failure.

**Why Agile Failed to Fix This:** The 2001 Agile Manifesto explicitly rejected "comprehensive documentation" in favor of "working software." Enterprise adoption (SAFe) simply repackaged waterfall planning into 8-10 week increments and called them Program Increments. The vocabulary changed. The overhead -- standups, sprint reviews, burndown charts, velocity tracking, backlog grooming, status slides delivered every two weeks instead of every two months -- did not.

**Where It Makes Sense:** Public-sector project tracking is defensible because federal agencies operate under the GAO's Yellow Book, requiring documentation of compliance with laws and regulations. The plan is a legal instrument protecting against Congressional hearings and contract termination. That is proportionate oversight of public funds. The trouble starts when private enterprises, accountable to shareholders (not appropriations committees), import this machinery wholesale and point it at projects whose only audience already knows the contents.

**What Actually Works:** Organizations that consistently deliver commit to dates they can keep, make them soon enough that missing one hurts, staff with people having both skill and decision authority, and escalate problems in hours rather than at next week's meeting. None of it requires a $24-per-seat tool with fifteen views and Salesforce integration. Most needs a shared document, a weekly conversation, and the nerve to say before month six that the plan was written by people who didn't yet know what they didn't know.

**The Scarce Resource:** Not software, not methodology -- organizational courage. The willingness to stand before people who approved the plan and declare it void. That nerve is what distinguishes successful integrations and deliveries. The apparatus substitutes for it, letting organizations be comprehensively informed about their own failure in real time, a genuine achievement that leaves the failure undisturbed.
